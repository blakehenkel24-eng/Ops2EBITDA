Customer concentration is a binary risk in private equity underwriting. A sponsor can love the unit economics, but if a single customer represents too much of the EBITDA, the entire deal thesis becomes fragile. Here's how PE firms calibrate it.

- **Revenue share thresholds.** Most sponsors get uncomfortable once a single customer exceeds **15% of revenue**, and the alarm rings above **20-25%**. At that level, the loss of the account would wipe out debt service coverage, which means lenders will cut leverage or require a springing covenant. The underwriting model always includes a "loss of key customer" scenario to test the downside.

- **Relationship stickiness vs. transactional risk.** A concentrated customer tied to a **multi-year contract with high switching costs** (e.g., embedded software, regulatory requirements) is less dangerous than a large, project-based customer that can re-bid annually. Sponsors map the renewal probability based on historical relationship length, decision-maker turnover, and the customer's own financial health. The risk is that a buyer at exit will still apply a subjective discount, regardless of contract terms, because the concentration screams "lumpy."

- **Exit multiple compression.** A business with a 20%+ customer concentration will typically trade at **1–3 turns of EBITDA lower** than a diversified peer. Strategic buyers hate dependency on one account, and PE buyers will build in a margin of safety. This directly reduces the sponsor's return, so the entry multiple must be lower to compensate.

- **Diligence focus.** The deal team will interview the customer's procurement team, analyze the wallet share, and assess whether the customer is itself a dis-synergistic buyer that could vertically integrate. They'll ask management: "If you lost this customer tomorrow, what's the bottom-up EBITDA bridge and how long to replace the lost gross profit?" The answer often kills the deal.

The concrete implication for underwriting: any concentration above 15% forces a **lower entry multiple, a greater equity cushion, and a mandatory post-close diversification plan** (often via add-ons or new channel development) to create a credible exit story. Without that path, the risk-adjusted return won't clear the investment committee.
