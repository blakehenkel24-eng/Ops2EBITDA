### chat-2-contextual: Contextual: Customer concentration in PE

<details><summary>Full output (378 words)</summary>

- **Revenue share thresholds.** Most sponsors start to get uncomfortable when a single customer exceeds **10-15% of revenue**, and anything above **25%** is typically a red flag that demands a significant valuation discount or a structured mitigation. The core fear: losing that customer could wipe out a third or more of EBITDA overnight, making the debt load unsustainable and crushing equity value. In practice, this often translates to a **1-3x turn discount** on the entry multiple relative to a diversified peer.

- **Diligence deep-dive.** PE firms don't just look at the percentage; they assess the *quality* of the concentration. Key questions: Is the relationship governed by a long-term contract with auto-renewals? How high are the customer's switching costs? Has the customer been growing or shrinking? Is the customer itself financially healthy? A 20% concentration with a sticky, multi-year government contract is far less risky than 20% with a single volatile retail chain. They also probe for any signs of backward integration risk.

- **Underwriting adjustments.** Sponsors will model a downside case where the top customer is lost entirely, testing whether the remaining business can still service debt and generate a minimum return. They may haircut the customer's EBITDA contribution by 50-100% in their base case, or apply a higher cost of equity. Earn-outs tied to customer retention, key-person insurance on the relationship owner, or a portion of the purchase price held in escrow are common structural mitigants.

- **Exit implications.** A concentrated customer base shrinks the pool of potential buyers. Strategic acquirers may already serve that customer and see no incremental value, or they may fear the same concentration risk. This can compress the exit multiple, so the entry multiple must be low enough to still deliver target returns even with a conservative exit. The second-order effect: a business that looks cheap on a headline multiple may actually be expensive once you adjust for the real risk-adjusted cash flows.

The concrete underwriting implication: a PE firm will set a **maximum entry multiple** that ensures an acceptable IRR even if the top customer departs in year two, often by capping the valuation at a level where the remaining, diversified EBITDA still yields a 3.0x MOIC over the hold period. If the seller won't accept that price, the deal dies.
