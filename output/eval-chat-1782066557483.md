<details><summary>Full output (378 words)</summary>

Customer concentration is one of the first things a sponsor flags in diligence because it directly attacks the defensibility of cash flows and exit multiples. A single customer representing **more than 15% of revenue** starts to compress valuation, and above **25%** many sponsors will pass unless there is a clear, near-term path to diversification.

Key underwriting lenses:

- **Revenue share thresholds.** Most sponsors get uncomfortable when the top customer exceeds **15-20%** of revenue. Above 25%, debt providers often begin to haircut EBITDA or tighten covenants, which directly reduces leverage capacity and returns. The fear is a binary event: lose that customer, and the equity story collapses.

- **Relationship durability.** The quality of the concentration matters as much as the percentage. Sponsors look for **sole-source contracts, mission-critical products, or high switching costs** that make the customer structurally dependent. A 30% customer that is a government entity on a 10-year take-or-pay contract is far less risky than a 20% customer that is a retail account with a 30-day cancellation clause.

- **Diversification path.** Underwriting must show a credible bridge to **sub-15% concentration within 2-3 years**. This might come from tuck-in acquisitions, new product launches, or geographic expansion. If the business has been top-heavy for years with no deceleration, sponsors assume the concentration is structural and penalize the multiple accordingly.

- **Multiple compression.** A business with clean customer diversification might trade at **10-12x EBITDA**, but a business with a 30% customer concentration often trades at **7-9x**, reflecting the risk of a cliff event. Lower entry multiples create a valuation overhang that makes it harder to generate target returns.

- **Mitigation in deal structure.** Sponsors might use **earn-outs tied to customer retention**, put insurance on key accounts, or require the seller to roll meaningful equity to align incentives. In some cases, a portion of the purchase price is contingent on the top customer renewing post-close.

The implication for underwriting: every deal model must stress-test a scenario where the top customer is lost entirely. If the business cannot survive that event without breaching covenants or requiring a fresh equity injection, the thesis is too fragile. Diligence must go beyond management’s comfort narrative and map the actual decision-making unit inside the customer, the renewal history, and any quiet threats that could break the relationship.
